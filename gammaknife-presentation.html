Definition

What Is the Gamma Knife?

  • A non-invasive neurosurgical device that delivers highly focused beams of gamma radiation to intracranial targets.
  • Despite the name, no knife or blade is used — the term refers to the knife-like precision of the radiation.
  • Manufactured by Elekta AB (Sweden), sold under the brand Leksell Gamma Knife®.
  • Gamma radiation comes from Cobalt-60 (⁶⁰Co) sources arranged in a helmet.
  • Each beam carries a sub-therapeutic dose; only at the convergence point does the cumulative dose become therapeutic.

Key principle:
Multiple low-dose beams intersect at a single focal point inside the brain, delivering a high, precise dose to the target while sparing surrounding healthy tissue.


Positioning:
The patient's head is immobilized in a stereotactic frame. MRI/CT images are fused to millimeter-accurate coordinates to plan the dose.

History

A Brief History

1951
Swedish neurosurgeon Lars Leksell introduces the concept of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS).
1968
First clinical Gamma Knife unit installed at the Karolinska Institute, Stockholm, Sweden.
1987
First unit outside Scandinavia installed at the University of Pittsburgh (USA), opening the technology to wider clinical use.
1990s
Rapid international expansion; indications broaden beyond AVMs to brain metastases, acoustic neuromas, and trigeminal neuralgia.
2009
Leksell Gamma Knife® Perfexion launched — robotic patient positioning, fully automatic helmet collimation.
2015–
Gamma Knife® Icon introduced: frameless mask option, on-board CBCT imaging, real-time motion management.

Technology

How It Works — Step by Step

🧠
1 · Imaging
High-resolution MRI, CT, or angiography identifies the target lesion with sub-millimeter accuracy.
📐
2 · Dose Planning
Treatment planning software (Leksell GammaPlan®) models beam angles, collimator sizes, and dose distribution.
🔒
3 · Fixation
A stereotactic frame or thermoplastic mask immobilizes the head, establishing a coordinate system.
☢️
4 · Irradiation
Up to 192 Co-60 beams simultaneously converge at the isocenter. Session typically lasts 15–60 minutes.

The radiation dose at the focus is typically 12–25 Gy in a single fraction, causing DNA double-strand breaks in the target cells while sparing adjacent healthy structures.

Clinical Applications

What Can It Treat?

Brain Metastases Most common indication; targets secondary tumors from lung, breast, melanoma, etc.
Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs) Radiation causes progressive obliteration of the AVM nidus over 2–3 years.
Acoustic Neuroma (Vestibular Schwannoma) High local control rates (≥95 % at 5 years) with hearing preservation.
Trigeminal Neuralgia Targets the trigeminal nerve root; pain relief in ~80 % of patients.
Meningioma Effective for small to medium skull-base or recurrent meningiomas.
Pituitary Adenoma Controls hormone-secreting or non-secreting adenomas not amenable to surgery.
Glioma (selected) Boost or recurrent high-grade glioma in selected cases.
Functional Disorders Obsessive-compulsive disorder, epilepsy (selected cases) via precise lesioning.

Clinical Outcomes

Evidence & Outcomes

  • Brain metastases: local control ~85–95 % at 1 year (JAMA Oncol., 2017).
  • Acoustic neuroma: tumor control ~95% at 10 years with a hearing preservation rate of 50–60%.
  • Trigeminal neuralgia: initial pain relief ~80–90%; recurrence ~30% at 3 years.
  • AVM obliteration: 80% obliteration for AVMs <3 cm at 3 years post-treatment.
  • Meningioma: 5-year local control >95% for grade I tumors.

Side Effects
• Acute: headache, nausea, fatigue (usually mild & transient).
• Subacute: radiation edema (~10–15 %), managed with corticosteroids.
• Late: radiation necrosis (<5 %), cranial nerve deficits (rare).
• Overall complication rate significantly lower than open surgery.

Conclusion

Key Takeaways

  • The Gamma Knife is the gold standard for stereotactic radiosurgery of intracranial lesions ≤3 cm.
  • It achieves surgical-level precision without opening the skull, dramatically reducing morbidity and recovery time.
  • Over 1.2 million patients have been treated since 1968, with a strong long-term evidence base.
  • The technology continues to evolve — frameless fixation, online image guidance, and AI-assisted planning are the frontier.
